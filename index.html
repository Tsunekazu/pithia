PITHIA

Independent research and experiments for Decred

What should Decred build next?

Pithia investigates what Decred could build, tests the promising ideas and publishes the evidence. Stakeholders vote on what comes first.

The point is to judge ideas on evidence before anyone commits time or treasury funds. AI agents do the digging and prototyping; a human editor reviews the evidence and marks what is still unresolved.

Investigation 01 · ScriptTested on a private network · reproduction invited

A covenant on today’s Decred

Can Decred’s existing script rules force coins to go to one exact place?

TakeawayYes, in a narrow form: today’s Decred script can lock coins to one exact destination, with no fork. That is a building block for theft-response vaults, not a vault yet.

What was tested · tested
A 196-byte script, built only from opcodes Decred already has, lets coins move to one fixed output and nowhere else. No fork, no new opcode. dcrd’s own script engine accepted it and rejected five tampered spends. On a private simnet running dcrd 2.1.7, a real spend passed standard mempool policy and was mined.
Strongest objection
It isn’t a vault. Amount and destination are fixed in advance, anyone can trigger the payment, and there is no recovery path, staking support or audit.
Why it matters · interpretation
Rules like this are the building block of theft-response vaults: withdrawals that wait out a delay and can be clawed back from cold storage.
Next useful test
A fixed-amount, two-stage vault on simnet: the hot key can only move coins to a staging output, a delay follows, and a cold key can recover during it. Then independent review. First, ask holders whether they want this.

How it works, limits & sources

How it works · interpretation

The script rebuilds the signature hash from a constrained transaction prefix, then checks a Schnorr signature it synthesises itself. It adapts Andrew Poelstra’s CAT-and-Schnorr tricks to Decred’s BLAKE-256 and Schnorr conventions.

Limits

Decred’s signature hash doesn’t commit to input value, so this is not an arbitrary-balance covenant. The search for prior art was bounded, so no claim of first discovery is made.

Still unknown · open

  • Full vault security
  • Flexible amounts & fees
  • Staking compatibility
  • Real demand

Investigation 02 · WalletsHypothesis · needs testing

Why can DCR backups fail in Cake Wallet?

And could one targeted fix make them reliable?

TakeawayCake’s own docs say large DCR wallets can break backups. The backup code suggests a likely cause, and it can be tested cheaply before anyone writes a fix.

What is known · sourced
Cake supports DCR (v6.5.0). Its own Decred docs say the wallet data makes backups very large and can make backup creation fail. Stakeholders approved Cake proposals in 2023 and 2025.
Strongest objection
We have not established how often DCR backups fail in practice, or how many people use DCR in Cake. A fix may already be planned.
Next useful test
Test whether the backup pipeline produces the suspected memory spike, using synthetic files and the exact library version Cake pins. Agree on acceptance criteria with a maintainer before writing any fix.
Hypothesis, unknowns & sources

What the source suggests · interpretation

The backup encrypts wallet data in chunks into one large file, then compresses it into a ZIP. The pinned ZIP encoder holds the compressed output in memory, so a big DCR wallet may run a phone out of memory.

Why it matters

Wallets drop coins that cost too much to maintain. Cake’s docs describe phasing out Zano, citing maintenance burden relative to usage.

Still unknown · open

  • Real failure rate
  • Peak memory on devices
  • DCR users in Cake
  • Already planned?

Sources

  • Cake docs · Decred

Investigation 03 · GovernanceComparing approaches

Easier polling for ticket holders

Can ticket holders answer quick, non-binding polls without unlocking a wallet every time?

TakeawayQuick ticket polls look feasible with the signing Decred already has. The one-click version risks linking mixed tickets, so privacy has to be answered first.

What is known · sourced
Politeia already verifies ticket votes, and interest in ticket-holder polling goes back at least to 2019. There are two ways to authorise an answer:
  • ASign every poll in your wallet, as Politeia votes are signed.
  • BA “voter passport”: sign once to authorise a browser key, then answer in one click.
Either could run on a Politeia-compatible server, so wallets that already vote could take part.
Strongest objection
Tying many tickets to one browser key could undo the privacy StakeShuffle mixing gives. And tickets vote after 28 days on average, so a passport tied to specific tickets would need frequent renewal.
Next useful test
On testnet, check whether existing wallet tools can sign a passport message with a ticket’s key, and count the steps. Compare both designs on privacy and effort before building anything.
Who benefits, unknowns & sources

Who would benefit

Stakeholders who want a say between big Politeia votes, without a wallet ritual for every small question.

Still unknown · open

  • Snapshot & signing support
  • Privacy of mixed tickets
  • Passport expiry rules
  • Demand for another poll

How it works

Make more ideas testable.

Decred’s research already lives across code, proposals, documentation and community discussion. Pithia would connect that work, investigate the open questions and turn promising leads into small experiments anyone can inspect.

  1. 01AI agents

    Investigate

    Agents read past Politeia proposals, the source code of wallets and nodes, and outside research, and run small tests where they can.

  2. 02Human editor

    Review

    An editor checks the evidence, marks what is unresolved and writes a short brief for each promising direction.

  3. 03Stakeholders

    Vote

    Stakeholders rank the shortlist and say what they would help with.

  4. 04Builders

    Test

    The top picks get small, cheap experiments. Results are published, good or bad.

Results feed the next round

Agents investigate and prototype. People review and decide.

Signals

For each direction, three separate questions.

Wanting something, helping with it and expecting it to work are different things. Most polls blur them together. Pithia asks them separately.

  • Lots of “yes, build it” and nobody willing to test it is phantom demand. Cheaper to spot before a proposal than after.
  • An unpopular idea with three committed testers might deserve a small experiment.
  • Forecasts get checked when the date arrives. Over time that becomes a public track record.

Stages

How Pithia could grow.

  1. Stage I · Now

    Oracle

    Investigate questions and publish the evidence: briefs, prototypes and results, in a regular digest in the spirit of the Decred Journal. On the web and on Bison Relay.

  2. Stage II · Next

    Poll

    Ticket holders signal priorities and say what they would help with, using the same tickets that vote on Politeia.

  3. Stage III · Later

    Steer

    Connect decisions to outcomes. Funded work is followed through, and the results inform the next decision.

Over time, Pithia could become a fuller governance platform. Whether it complements Politeia or succeeds it would be a separate decision for stakeholders, made through Politeia.

Ground rules

What Pithia will and won’t do.

  1. Agents investigate. People decide.Agents dig and prototype. An editor reviews. Stakeholders choose.
  2. Every claim is labelled.Sourced, tested, interpreted or open. Original research isn’t a guess just because nobody else has published it.
  3. Reviewer time is part of the budget.Maintainers get a runnable result and a scoped question, never a pile of raw AI output.
  4. Signals advise. Politeia decides.Nothing here moves treasury funds.
  5. The editor’s choices are visible.For now Jure picks what gets investigated, from community suggestions. Every shortlist says why each item is on it; corrections and dead ends are published too.
  6. No new token. No accounts. No tracking.This page runs no JavaScript and loads nothing from third parties.
